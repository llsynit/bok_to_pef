Present simple – vanlig presens

På engelsk bruker vi vanlig presens for å fortelle om noe som skjer regelmessig, noe vi pleier å gjøre, og tilstander som ikke endrer seg.

  • They leave early every morning.
  • I play football on Tuesdays.
  • Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.

Present continuous – presens samtidsform

På engelsk bruker vi presens samtidsform for å fortelle om noe som skjer akkurat nå, og som skal avsluttes på et tidspunkt. Vi danner presens samtidsform slik: vanlig presens av hjelpeverbet to be + -ing-form av hovedverbet.

  • They are leaving right now.

    (Meaning: They will be gone in a minute.)

  • She is travelling the world.

    (Meaning: She will come home in two months.)

  • I am boiling an egg.

    (Meaning: The egg will be done soon.)

My dream destination (Model text)

I dream about going to the Bahamas. I want to travel by boat from island to island, swim in crystal clear water and enjoy the tropical climate. I do not like the cold, and in the Bahamas they never have winters like we do.

If you look at the map, you will see that the Bahamas consists of lots of islands. In fact, there are more than 700 islands that are part of the Bahamas. They are situated in the Atlantic Ocean south-east of Florida, USA. The capital, Nassau, is located on the island of New Providence. This island has the biggest population. Most Bahamians have ancestors from West Africa. English is the official language, because the country once was an English colony. Today it is an independent state.

The islands have some of the world's most beautiful beaches with soft, white sand and turquoise water. People who travel to the Bahamas often come for the wonderful climate. They enjoy the spectacular beaches and relax in the sun.

Some also dive amongst the coral reefs, swim with sharks and explore caves. Others visit the national parks to see the turtles and the exotic birds. If you spend Christmas there, you can experience Junkanoo on 26 December. Junkanoo is a street parade that resembles a carnival. The locals wear costumes, play goat skin drums and cow bells and dance through the streets.

The Bahamas also has wonderful food which includes lots of seafood, tropical fruits and spices. I really hope I one day will travel to the Bahamas.

Hannah, Norway

Travelling words

Before reading

Look at the title and the illustrations. What do you think this text might be about?

You have probably heard about how the English language has spread across the world. But did you know that the Vikings once influenced the English language? In fact, the Vikings exported many words from Old Norse to England when they first travelled there in the Viking Age.

At the beginning of the year 793 AD, the Vikings attacked the monastery at Lindisfarne in the north-east of England. The monks were brutally killed or enslaved. The Vikings stole all the treasures and damaged the monastery. This incident has traditionally been used to mark the beginning of the Viking Age (793–1066 AD). However, not all Vikings travelled to steal, burn and kill during the Viking Age. Most of them were probably just looking for fertile land or someone to trade goods with.

The Vikings were also known as the Norsemen. Many of them first settled on the northern and eastern shores of England.

In this area, many of the villages and cities have names that end with the Norse words by and torp, for example Grimsby and Scunthorpe. Grim, for example, was a common Norse name and Grimsby simply means 'Grim's village'. Norse words such as husbondi, knifr and taka are words that are still used in English, although these words have changed over time.

Simply put, the Vikings were great shipbuilders and travelled far across the seas. On these travels, they took the Old Norse language with them. It is quite fascinating that English, the world language of today, still carries traces of the Vikings, don't you agree?

How to make a fortune-teller (Model text)

Follow the steps below to make a fortune-teller.

For this, you will need a square piece of paper.

  • Step 1: Fold the piece of paper from corner to corner to make a triangle.

  • Step 2: Fold the triangle from corner to corner to make a smaller triangle. Crease well!

  • Step 3: Unfold it completely. Now you have a paper with an X-shaped crease.

  • Step 4: Fold each corner into the centre of the X. Now you have a smaller square. Flip your paper over.

  • Step 5: Fold each corner back into the centre of the smaller square. Now you have an even smaller square.

  • Step 6: Use both hands and place your index fingers and thumbs under each of the flaps underneath.
  • Step 7: Push gently upwards to create a diamond-shaped fortune-teller.

  • Step 8: Carefully open the centre of the fortune-teller so it is flat.

  • Step 9: Colour the four flaps on the outside in four different colours.

  • Step 10: Number each flap on the inside with numbers from 1 to 8.

  • Step 11: Write eight different instructions or tasks on the inside-flaps of the fortune-teller. Write your instructions in the imperative.

Example:

Give somebody a big smile at recess!

Sing the chorus of your favourite song!

Plastic pollution causes great damage to the aquatic life because fish and seabirds eat the toxic plastic pieces, leaving no room in their bellies for food, or get trapped in plastic bags and nets.

The promising solution

The Japanese experiment

A brief history of London

Before reading

What do you know about London? Freewrite for two minutes.

Before the Romans came, only a few Celtic tribes lived in villages spread along the river Thames. For more than 2000 years, various groups of people have contributed to the growth of what we know today as the metropolis of London.

In 43 AD the Roman emperor, Claudius, invaded the southern part of Britain and made it part of The Roman Empire. Seven years later, the Romans built a town where the river Thames was narrow enough to build a bridge. They called the town Londinium. To defend their town, they built a wall around it. You can still see traces of this wall today. In today's London, the small part of the city that lies within these old walls is called "The City of London".

By 410 AD most Romans had left Londinium, and the Anglo Saxons came to southern England. The Anglo Saxons mostly came from the countries we know today as Denmark, the Netherlands and Germany.

They built a town just one mile from Londinium and called it Lundenwic. The two towns later joined. The old Roman walls gave protection when Vikings raided the city.

Many monarchs have ruled England, but the story of the Tudor family has fascinated people for centuries. The Tudors ruled for 118 years. Their most famous king, Henry viii, married six times. He divorced two of his wives and had two beheaded. One wife died, but his final wife outlived him. In the beginning of the Tudor period, London had 50 000 inhabitants. Trade grew and London blossomed. By 1605 the city's population had risen to 225 000 people. The city was overcrowded and the living conditions were poor for many Londoners.

One event that affected the city during this period was the Great Fire in 1666. It started in a bakery, spread very quickly and lasted for four days. People escaped out on the Thames in boats and many fled to the fields outside the city. More than 100 000 people were left homeless and it took more than 50 years to rebuild London. Between 1750 and 1850, the population of Britain more than doubled. During this time, more people were needed in the cities to work in the factories, and people poured into London by the thousands.

The Second World War was hard on Londoners. The city was frequently bombed and the more than eight million inhabitants lived in constant fear. During the bombing of London, which was called "the Blitz", many children were sent to the countryside so they wouldn't be killed. When the war ended in 1945, the bombs had killed thousands of Londoners and destroyed many homes. Luckily, several of the famous landmarks in London were not damaged.

Today, London is a multicultural city with almost nine million inhabitants speaking more than 300 languages. It is the most-visited city in the world if you measure by international arrivals. Few places can offer so much history, so many different cultures, famous landmarks, sports, fantastic culture and shopping – all within the same city.

An old rhyme tells of the fate of Henry the viii's six wives: Divorced, beheaded, died. Divorced, beheaded, survived.

One year before the fire, the great plague had killed almost a fifth of London's population. During the fire, the unhygienic city slums burned down and this brought the plague under control.

The children of the Industrial Revolution

Before reading

Think about the following: What was life like for most children in Europe in the 1800s?

Queen Victoria ruled Britain from 1837 to 1901. During the Victorian era, Britain became the biggest empire the world has seen, ruling over one fifth of the world. Although this made Britain wealthy, it only benefitted a few. The British colonists suppressed the people native to the areas they colonised. And at home life was hard for most Britons.

During the Industrial Revolution people moved from the countryside to the cities to work in the factories and the coal mines.

The cities grew rapidly and the British population increased from 10.5 million in 1801 to 30.5 million in 1901. People lived cramped together in the city slums. Most people of the working classes received very low wages, and even small children had to work to make ends meet.

In the Victorian era, it was common to have as many as ten children. There were several reasons for this. Firstly, there was no access to birth control. Secondly, many children didn't even survive childhood. About twenty per cent did not see their sixth birthday because of diseases like cholera, tuberculosis and smallpox, which meant for a very painful death. Having many children was a way to make sure that some would grow up. Also, more children meant more workers that could support the family. Many children started working as early as the age of five.

Children could do many different jobs. Some worked in the coal mines. They pushed trolleys in the depths of the mines for as long as eighteen hours a day. A few were lucky, and worked outside sorting the coal. Many other children worked in the factories. To the factory owners they were cheap and useful labour. They had tiny fingers, which made it possible for them to do things adults found difficult, and they could crawl under the big machines. Some children also worked as chimney sweepers. As they were small, they could crawl up and down the chimneys more easily. Many children became very ill and even died from all the fumes, the dust and the chemicals they were exposed to.

During the Victorian era, only children from the upper classes attended school. When Queen Victoria was crowned, one third of the British population could not read or write. But, the British Empire was growing, and politicians realised they needed educated people. In 1870, the government introduced schooling for all children under the age of 13. However, it took a long time before children stopped working.

The Industrial Revolution was a time of great change that started in the 18th century. Many important inventions were made that revolutionised society. These changes meant that goods were no longer hand crafted in people's homes, but rather mass-produced in big factories.

In the 19th century, life seemed harsh and hopeless to many people in the slums of London. Because of the cramped living conditions, diseases spread quickly. In 1854, an outbreak of cholera killed many people in the Soho district in London, but as it turned out, this wasn't necessarily because people were living closely together. The outbreaks of cholera were difficult to prevent, but sometimes, the solution to a problem may be easier than you think.

In London in the mid-19th century, most doctors believed that diseases such as cholera spread through what they called "bad air". Cholera caused diarrhoea and vomiting, which led to dehydration and often death for the people who were infected. The physician John Snow was sceptical about the theory of "bad air". He found it peculiar that so many people in the same area got the disease, while people in other parts of London were hardly affected. Surely the air would move outside this area?

Dr Snow decided to map out the cases of cholera, and with a map of Soho he started walking around, marking the addresses where the illness was found. Snow soon realized that most deaths from the disease were located around Broad Street, where the neighbourhood water pump was located. He suspected that the water from the pump helped spread the illness. Those who got water from other pumps farther away, were not affected like the inhabitants of Soho.

Although he could not prove his theory by testing water samples, his map and argumentation was good enough for the local council to remove the handle of the pump. The cholera outbreak was already on decline, but shutting down the water pump drastically reduced the cases of cholera. It was later discovered that the spring below the pump was too close to the sewage, and that this must have been how the cholera bacteria got into the drinking water.

Dr Snow proved that water can be a source of illness, and that having clean drinking water is important to prevent illness from spreading. Today, we have vaccines to prevent cholera and medicine to cure it. However, as clean water, vaccines and medicines are not available to everyone in the world, people in developing countries still die from this preventable disease.

A colony of convicts (Model text)

In Britain in the 18th century, there was a lot of poverty. The justice system was harsh and unfair. A minor crime, like stealing food, could place you in prison for years. As a result, the prisons became overcrowded. The authorities had to come up with a solution. The answer to the problem would be on the other side of the world.

In 1770, the British captain James Cook arrived in Australia. First, he claimed the eastern part of Australia as British territory. Then, the British started building a colony under the leadership of Captain Arthur Phillip. Meanwhile, the authorities prepared the system of "Transportation". Prisoners were sent across the world to serve their sentences in the new British colony in Australia.

In 1787, the first fleet of eleven ships started the journey from England to New South Wales. More than 700 convicts were on board the ships. They were heading for the first British penal colony in Australia, Rose Hill. Many of the convicts had been arrested for petty crimes and some were political prisoners. During the crossing, the prisoners spent most of their time below deck. Finally, after eight months at sea, the fleet reached Port Jackson.

The convicts, who were sent into exile, helped build the British colony. They had to serve their time doing hard physical labour in the new colony. Male convicts had to build roads, construct buildings, make bricks and mine for coal.

Female convicts also had to work hard, usually as servants or as farm labourers. Convicts who were lucky enough to have been educated were often given easier work. Meanwhile, the transportation of convicts to the new colony continued for many years to come.

Finally, in 1868, the last ship arrived in Western Australia and unloaded the last 269 convicts. Over a period of 80 years, a total number of around 165,000 men and women had been transported. When the transportation ended, convicts and ex-convicts made up 40 percent of Australia's English-speaking population.

As time went on, convicts who were trusted with some freedom were given a so-called "ticket of leave", which allowed them to work for themselves. However, most of them did not have the freedom to actually leave the colony. Today, approximately 20 percent of the population in Australia are descendants of these early convicts.

This is the story of how Maui caught the Great Fish of New Zealand. When I was very young I imagined that Maui was very young too and I was very impressed that such a young boy could catch such a great fish.

When I look at my maps of New Zealand, I can see that it is in fact the North Island that is in the shape of a fish. However, I always imagined it was shaped like a shark, not a fish, because when I first heard the story I was terrified of sharks and thought that the shark was the only fish that would fight so violently against Maui's god-like powers.

The Maori name for the North Island is still Te Ika a Maui, meaning the fish of Maui.

Maui was, they say, half man and half god. He knew many magic spells and had many magic powers that his older brothers didn't know about, or if they did they pretended to ignore.

One day, when he heard his brothers talking about going fishing, Maui decided that he wanted to go too. So, before his brothers had woken up he went down to where the canoe was, carrying his special fishing hook.

Hearing his brothers approach he quickly hid under the floorboards of the boat.

The brothers arrived and they were laughing about having managed to escape without Maui. They were looking forward to having a good day's fishing without being bothered by their younger brother.

They pushed out from shore and were still laughing when suddenly they heard a noise.

'What was that?' asked one of them.

Then they thought they heard someone talking. They couldn't see anyone; they couldn't see anything except for the water.

'Oh, it must have been a seagull or something screeching in the distance,' suggested another of the brothers.

Then they heard the sound again. It was Maui, laughing and saying in a strange voice, 'I am with you. You haven't tricked me at all.'

The brothers were becoming quite scared now. It sounded like muffled speech but there was no one to be seen.

On they paddled into deep waters. Again they heard the voice and this time one of the brothers said he thought the noise was coming from under the floorboards so he wrenched up a few. There was Maui, laughing loudly and boasting, 'I tricked you! I tricked you!'

The brothers were amazed to see Maui there. They decided to turn back immediately. 'You are not coming with us,' they said. 'You're far too young and our father doesn't want you to come with us.'

But Maui said, 'Look back! Look back to the land! Look how far away it is!'

He had used his magic powers to make the land seem much further away than it really was. The brothers, not realizing that it was a trick, reluctantly agreed to take Maui with them.

They paddled on for a while and then stopped. Just as they were about to throw over the anchor and start their fishing, Maui said, 'No. Please don't do that because I know a much better place further out, full of fish – all the fish you could want. Just a little while longer and you'll have all your nets filled in half the time.'

The brothers were tempted by this promise of fish and paddled out a little further when Maui stopped them and told them to start fishing. So they threw over their nets which within a few minutes were overflowing. They couldn't believe their luck.

Their boat was lying low in the water with the weight of their catch so the brothers told Maui that they were going to turn back. But Maui said, 'No, it's my turn. I haven't had a chance to do my fishing.'

'But we have enough!' they replied.

'No, I want to do my fishing,' insisted Maui.

With that, he pulled out his special fishing hook made of bone, and asked for some bait. The brothers refused to give him any so Maui rubbed his nose so hard that it began to bleed. Then he smeared the hook with his own blood and threw it over the side.

Suddenly the boat was tossed about, and Maui was thrilled because he was sure he had caught a very big fish. He pulled and pulled. The sea was in a turmoil and Maui's brothers sat in stunned silence marvelling at Maui's magic strength.

Maui heaved and tugged for what seemed like an age until at last the fish broke the surface. Then Maui and his brothers could see that what he had caught was not a fish, but a piece of land, and that his hook was embedded in the doorway of the house of Tonganui, the son of the Sea God.

Maui's brothers couldn't believe their eyes. This beautiful land pulled up from the sea, was smooth and bright, and there were houses on it and burning fires and birds singing. They had never seen anything so marvellous in their whole lives.

Realizing what he had done Maui said, 'I must go and make peace with the gods because I think they are very angry with me. Stay here quietly and calmly, until I return.'

As soon as Maui had gone the brothers forgot his instructions and began to argue for possession of the land.

'I want this piece,' said one.

'No! I claimed it first. It's mine!' shouted another.

Soon the brothers began to slash at the land with their weapons. This angered the gods even more and its smooth surface was gashed and cut. It could never be smoothed out again.

To this day, those cuts and bruises of long ago can still be seen in the valleys and mountains of New Zealand.

The powhiri is a Maori ceremony of encounter and involves both chanting, singing (waiata) and dancing (haka). Traditionally the ceremony was meant to determine whether visitors were friends or enemies. Once it was confirmed that the visitors had friendly intentions, the powhiri became a formal welcoming of guests. Here are the words chanted and sung by the hosts, or "home people", during the powhiri:

Be alert! Be watchful! Be alert on this rampart!

Be alert on that rampart! Be watchful! Be alert!

O the canoes. The four winds. Great ones. The tribes.

Talking birds of the marae.

Listen! Listen! Listen!

Listen to the cry of the bird calling "Unite, unite, be one!"

Unite above, unite below, unite within,

Unite without, unite in the brotherhood of man.

The night hears, the night hears

Unite the descent lines from Great Hawaiki

From long Hawaiki, from Hawaiki far away

Joined to the spirit, to the daylight, to the world of light.

To the peoples of the whole world

Welcome, welcome, welcome.

Welcome to the land of Aotearoa

Welcome to the world of the Maori.

This is my greeting to you, all the peoples of the world

This is my greeting of love to you all.

In Maori mythology, Hawaiki is the original home of the Maori people, before they travelled across the sea to New Zealand in canoes. In many Maori stories, Hawaiki is also the name of the underworld.

The Stolen Generations (Further reading)

Before reading

When the British arrived in Botany Bay in 1770, they settled on the fertile east coast and founded the British colony in Australia. The Aboriginal peoples, the indigenous people of Australia, had been living there for thousands of years. Now their land and their hunting grounds were taken from them. Eventually, many Aboriginal families also lost their children.

"The Stolen Generations" is an expression used to describe the Australian Aboriginal children that British authorities stole from their parents. In the years between 1905 and 1969, thousands of Aboriginal children were taken from their homes by the government. They were brought up in institutions or taken to live with white foster families and taught to speak only English. Many never saw their families again. They lost their Aboriginal names and were punished if they spoke their own language.

When the British arrived in Australia, the Aboriginal population declined very quickly. Many were killed and many died from European diseases. The popular excuse was that the Australian Aboriginal people were inferior to the Northern Europeans. They would naturally die out in modern Australia. But children of mixed race were a dilemma. These children had one white parent and one Aboriginal parent. What should the new Australian society do with these children? The solution was brutal: to take them away from their families and raise them in a European environment. In this way, the children would forget about their Aboriginal origin. They could be "white washed" into the Australian society.

The stolen Aboriginal children received very little education. They were expected to work as manual workers and domestic servants in white families. As the children grew up, they experienced racism from white Australians. This is not surprising when we consider this: until 1967, Australian laws stated that "Aboriginal natives" should be left out when counting the population of Australia.

Between 1910 and 1970, about 50,000 Aboriginal children were separated from their families. In 1998, the first National Sorry Day was held to remember how the country's indigenous population had been treated. In 2008, Kevin Rudd, the Prime Minister of Australia at the time, gave an apology to the stolen generations for tearing apart families and forcing them to adapt to white Australia.

How to write a story

Before reading

Think about your favourite story, either written or filmed. What do you love about this story? Freewrite for two minutes about what makes this story so good.

Sometimes coming up with a good story can be difficult. Although you are free to use your imagination, it can be helpful to have some guidelines to get you started. Here are some tips about what a story should include:

Character

First, you need a main character. Think about what kind of person (or animal or alien) this is. Is your main character shy, clumsy or heroic? Are there other important characters in your story? What should they be like? The better the reader knows your characters, the more engaged they will be in your story. The best way to show what a character is like, is through the way they act, the things they do and what they say.

Setting

The setting is the frame for your story. When you start writing, you have to figure out where you would like the story to take place. You also have to decide when your story takes place. Maybe everything happens in the main character's kitchen, or maybe you want to leave planet Earth all together? Does your story take place in the future, or did it take place a few hours ago?

Problem

Almost all stories have one thing in common: there is a problem that needs to be solved. The problem can be tiny, like a horrible pimple on your nose the day of your school photo. Or the problem can be big, like aliens attacking your school. Decide what your character's problem is before you start writing. Also, decide how the problem should be solved, although you may change this as the story develops.

The opening lines of your story should make the reader interested in reading the rest of the story. Short stories often start in the middle of the action. We call this technique in medias res. Here are three different ways of starting in the middle of the action:

Infinitiv (The infinitive)

Infinitiv er grunnformen av et verb. Det er den formen du vil finne i ordlister. Av og til brukes infinitivsmerket foran grunnformen. På norsk er å infinitivsmerket, på engelsk brukes to:

  • å snakke – to talk
  • å reise – to travel

Vanlig presens (Present simple)

Vanlig presens brukes for å fortelle om noe som skjer regelmessig, noe vi pleier å gjøre og tilstander som ikke endrer seg.

  • They leave early every morning.
  • I play football on Tuesdays.
  • Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.

Samsvarsbøying (Concord)

I vanlig presens må det være samsvar (concord) mellom subjektet og verbalet i setningen. Vanlig presens er alltid lik infinitiven, men vi legger til -s når subjektet i setningen er he, she og it eller andre ord som kan erstattes med he, she og it: my father, Jenny, the cat

  • to wish – he wishes

Hvis verbets grunnform slutter på y med konsonant foran, forandres y til i, og vi legger til -es i 3. person entall:

  • to reply – Mary replies

Presens samtidsform (Present continuous)

Presens samtidsform brukes for å fortelle om noe som skjer akkurat nå, om en pågående prosess eller noe som snart skal skje.

Presens samtidsform dannes ved hjelpeverbet to be + -ing-form av hovedverbet:

Eksempler:

  • They are leaving right now. (noe som skjer nå)
  • I am studying at the University. (en pågående prosess)
  • She is coming tomorrow. (nær framtid)

write wrote written skrive

Preteritum samtidsform (Past continuous)

Ing-formen i preteritum blir ofte brukt for å uttrykke en handling som pågikk da noe annet skjedde.

Vi danner preteritum samtidsform slik: preteritum av hjelpeverbet to be + -ingform av hovedverbet:

  • I was taking a shower when the phone rang.
  • While Jenny and Rick were making dinner, the dog ran away.
  • Det som skjedde underveis, uttrykkes ved hjelp av vanlig preteritum (rang, ran).

Vi bruker ellers ing-form i preteritum for å uttrykke to handlinger som foregikk samtidig, eller for å få fram at handlingen varte en stund:

  • Mum was sleeping while I was doing the dishes.

    (handlinger som foregikk samtidig)

  • We were playing video games all afternoon.

    (handling som varte en stund)

Presens perfektum (Present perfect)

Presens perfektum forteller at noe har hendt.

Presens perfektum dannes av hjelpeverbet to have + perfektum partisipp av hovedverbet:

  • She has played football.
  • We have seen the film.

For alle regelrette verb er perfektum partisipp lik preteritum (dvs. slutter på -ed), mens uregelrette verb har sin egen form:

  • We have bought a horse.
  • I have swum in the sea.

Se liste over uregelrette verb på s. 246.

Presens futurum (Future simple)

Presens futurum forteller at noe skal skje. Her er to vanlige måter å uttrykke presens futurum på:

  • will + infinitiv

    Denne måten bruker vi når vi vil uttrykke at noe kommer til å skje, uavhengig av om vi har planlagt det eller ikke.

    • I will see you next week.
    • It will rain tomorrow.

  • be going to + infinitiv:

    Denne måten bruker vi når vi vil uttrykke at man har bestemt seg for å gjøre noe eller har tenkt å gjøre noe:

    • I am going to clean my room on Friday.
    • We are going to rent a house on the coast this summer.

Imperativ (The imperative)

Imperativ er den formen vi bruker når vi for eksempel gir ordre, oppfordrer eller instruerer. Vi danner imperativ av infinitivsformen av verbet uten infinitivsmerket to:

  • Eat! Sit down! Come here! (ordre)
  • Please come in and sit down. (oppfordring)
  • Take a left and then a right. (instruksjon)

For å lage nektende setninger legger vi til do not foran verbet:

  • Do not boil the potatoes too long.

Hjelpeverb (Auxiliary verbs)

Hjelpeverbene to be, to have og to do blir som oftest brukt for å danne tider av verb:

  • I am helping you. I was helping you.
  • He has done his homework. He had done his homework.
  • Do you play football? Did you play football?

Modale hjelpeverb (Modal auxiliary verbs)

Modale hjelpeverb brukes for å uttrykke for eksempel mulighet, tillatelse, plikt, evne, hensikt og vilje. Disse verbene varierer innholdet i hovedverbet. Vi har følgende modale hjelpeverb:

  • can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would, ought to

Modale hjelpeverb har kun én form, uansett subjekt, og de følges alltid av en infinitiv:

  • He may sit here.
  • She should visit her grandparents.
  • It will get better.

Passiv form av verbet (The passive voice)

I en aktiv setning utfører subjektet i setningen handlingen:

  • Mary (S) painted (V) the house this summer.

I en passiv setning utfører ikke subjektet i setningen handlingen, men noe blir gjort med subjektet:

  • The house (S) was painted (V) this summer by Mary.

Den som utfører handlingen, kan være nevnt i setningen (... by Mary). Vi bruker alltid preposisjonen by for å uttrykke dette. Noen ganger er det ikke viktig å vite hvem som utfører handlingen. Da sløyfes dette leddet:

  • English is spoken here.
  • Books are sold in the bookstore.

Passiv form av verbet brukes oftere på engelsk enn på norsk.

2. Substantiv (Nouns)

Substantiv er ord for personer, steder, ting og dyr. Abstrakte ord, for eksempel «en tanke», er også et substantiv. Vi deler substantivene inn i egennavn (proper nouns) og fellesnavn (common nouns).

Regelrett bøying

Vi lager flertall ved å legge til -s til de fleste substantiv:

  • room – rooms
  • book – books

-es legges til de fleste til ord som slutter på en s-lyd (vislelyd), dvs. ord som slutter på -s, -ch og -sh:

  • class – classes
  • watch – watches
  • dish – dishes

-es legges til ord som slutter på -y med konsonant foran. Da forandres y til i + -es i flertall:

  • lady – ladies
  • country – countries

En del substantiv som i entall slutter på -o, får lagt til -es i flertall:

  • hero – heroes
  • potato – potatoes

Eksempler på unntak: Piano, photo og radio får endingen -s:

pianos, photos, radios

En del substantiv som i entall slutter på -f eller -fe, får endingen -ves i flertall:

  • thief – thieves
  • half – halves
  • knife – knives
  • wife – wives

Eksempler på unntak: Roof, chief, handkerchief får endingen -s:

roofs, chiefs, handkerchiefs

Uregelrett bøying

Noen substantiv har egen flertallsform, og vokallyden endres fra entall til flertall. Her er noen av dem:

EntallFlertall
woman women
man men
child children
foot feet
tooth teeth
mouse mice

Noen substantiv har samme form i entall og flertall:

  • a sheep – many sheep
  • a deer – many deer
  • a fish – many fish

Substantiv som alltid er entall

Eksempler: news, money, furniture, advice, information, homework, milk, bread, luggage, pollution. Verb knyttet til disse substantivene står alltid i entall:

  • No news is good news.
  • Where is my money? It is on the table.
  • This furniture looks old.

Substantiv som alltid er flertall

Eksempler: police, people, clothes, trousers, stairs, headphones, directions. Verb knyttet til disse substantivene står alltid i flertall.

  • These people are all members of our club.
  • The police were at the scene of the crime.
  • His trousers are blue.

Genitiv (The genitive)

Genitiv brukes for å uttrykke eierskap, at noe tilhører noen.

  • Genitiv med apostrof + s ('s)

    Denne formen brukes ofte når «eieren» er en person eller et dyr:

    • My friend's mother called yesterday.
    • I just love Mary's new coat.
    • The cat's eyes shone in the dark room.

    I flertall legger vi vanligvis bare til apostrof: The brothers' house.

    Ved uregelrett flertallsform der substantivet ikke slutter på s, må vi legge til apostrof og s: The children's playground.

  • Genitiv med of

    Denne formen brukes ofte når «eieren» ikke er en person eller et dyr:

    • The capital of New Zealand is Wellington.
    • The photographer captured the beauty of the sunset.

3. Adjektiv (Adjectives)

Adjektiv er ord som beskriver mennesker, dyr, ting og ideer:

a smart girl, beautiful kittens, a tall tree, complete freedom

Gradbøying (Comparison)

Adjektiv blir ofte brukt for å uttrykke graden av noe. De blir gradbøyd i positiv, komparativ og superlativ.

  • Adjektiv som består av én stavelse gradbøyes med -er og -est. Det gjør også adjektiv på to stavelser som slutter på -y, -er eller -ow.

    PositivKomparativSuperlativ
    (Enstavelse) small smaller the smallest
    (Tostavelse) clever cleverer cleverest

    Legg merke til at adjektiv som slutter på -y, endrer vokal til -i, for eksempel

    easy – easier – easiest

    Adjektiv som slutter på -le får kun endingen -r og -st, for eksempel

    little – littler – littlest

  • Andre adjektiv gradbøyes med more og most

    • beautiful – more beautiful – the most beautiful

  • Noen adjektiv har en egen bøying som må læres, for eksempel:

    • good – better – the best
    • bad – worse – the worst
    • ill – worse – the worst

    Ofte har vi behov for å forsterke adjektivene. Det gjøres slik:

    • very happy – much happier – the very happiest
    • very good – much better – the very best

Nasjonalitetsadjektiv (Adjectives of nationality)

Adjektivet blir brukt for å fortelle om nasjonaliteten til en person eller ting og hvilket språk personen snakker:

Norwegian salmon is popular all over the world. Ola speaks Norwegian.

4. Adverb (Adverbs)

Adverbet sier noe mer om verbet i setningen: hvordan noe gjøres (måtesadverb), når eller hvor ofte noe skjer (tidsadverb), og hvor noe foregår eller er (stedsadverb):

  • She sings beautifully. (hvordan hun synger)
  • My brother came home yesterday. (når broren kom hjem)
  • We sometimes watch TV in the morning. (hvor ofte vi ser på tv)
  • Jillian is sitting outside. (hvor Jillian sitter)
  • My book is here on the table. (hvor boka er)

  • Adverb på -ly:

    AdjektivAdverb
    beautiful beautifully
    slow slowly

    Adverb på -ly gradbøyes med more og most.

    • happily – more happily – most happily
    • terribly – more terribly – most terribly

    NB! Early gradbøyes med -er og -est: early – earlier – earliest

  • Adverb og adjektiv med samme form

    Eksempler: daily, weekly, monthly, fast, long, hard, late, near

    AdjektivAdverb
    The car is fast. The car runs fast.
    Ellen works hard. Ellen is a hard worker.

  • good – well

    Til adjektivet good hører adverbet well:

    • You are a good singer. You sing well.

  • Verb som ikke følges av adverb

    Etter sanseverbene taste, smell, feel, look skal vi ikke bruke adverb, men adjektiv. Det samme gjelder etter verbene seem og sound.

    • This pasta dish smells really good.
    • That suitcase looks heavy.
    • Your plans for the future sound exciting.

5. Pronomen (Pronouns)

Pronomen er ord som står istedenfor andre ord.

Personlige pronomen (Personal pronouns)

Personlige pronomen brukes istedenfor substantiv:

  • Gary likes Emma.
  • He (Gary) likes her (Emma).

Det er to former for personlige pronomen, avhengig av om de er subjekt eller objekt i setningen.

SubjektObjekt

2. person you you
3. person he him

Flertall 1. person we us
2. person you you
3. person they them
  • I helped Peter. / Peter helped me.
  • He is nice. / Do you like him?

Refleksive pronomen (Reflexive pronouns)

Refleksive pronomen peker tilbake til subjektet i samme setning:

I myself
you yourself
he himself
she herself
it itself
we ourselves
you yourselves
they themselves

Eksempler:

  • I cut myself on the glass.
  • We introduced ourselves.
  • Did you hurt yourself?
  • Did you enjoy yourselves?
  • He prepared himself for the test.
  • They dried themselves off.

Pronomenet «det» (The pronoun "it")

Det er ulike måter for å uttrykke «det» på engelsk.

  • Vi bruker it når vi snakker om vær, avstand, temperatur og tid.

    • It was snowing yesterday.
    • It is five kilometres to the supermarket.
    • It was 20 degrees Celsius in the water.
    • It is 10 a.m.

  • Vi bruker there is/are når vi kan si at noe fins eller er til stede. Vi bruker is når det vi refererer til, er i entall, og are når det er i flertall.

    • There is one boy in my group.
    • There are many people in the world.

Spørrepronomen (Interrogative pronouns)

Spørrepronomenene who, which, what, whose og whom brukes i spørrende setninger:

  • What is your name?
  • Who is your sister?
  • Which car is yours?
  • Whose book is this?
  • To whom did you speak? (formelt språk)

Subjunksjonen som

Subjunksjonen som kan hete who, which eller that på engelsk, alt etter hva den viser til.

  • Who viser bare til mennesker.

    James Cook, who discovered Australia, was British.

  • Which viser bare til dyr og ting.

    London, which is the capital of the UK, has many inhabitants.

    Fido, which is my brother's dog, ran away yesterday.

  • That kan brukes om mennesker, dyr og ting.

    The person that discovered Australia was British.

7. Preposisjoner (Prepositions)

Preposisjoner er ord som forteller om hvor noe er i forhold til noe annet, for eksempel mellom, bak, under. Preposisjoner uttrykker også tid, for eksempel om, i, før.

En fin måte å lære seg preposisjonene på, er å legge merke til hvordan de er brukt i fraser og setninger.

Preposisjoner som uttrykker tid (Prepositions of time)

Eksempler: in, for, during, since, before, until, at, on

  • I'll see you in an hour.
  • We have been sitting here since noon.
  • Leave the keys on the table before you leave.
  • He was born at 9.15 a.m. on 5 January.

Andre preposisjoner som også blir brukt om tid:

  • about, after, ago, between, by, from

Preposisjoner som uttrykker bevegelse (Prepositions of movement)

Eksempler: to, from, into, out of

  • They went to a football match.
  • He came from England.
  • We walked into the restaurant.
  • She walked out of the shopping mall.

Preposisjoner som uttrykker sted (Prepositions of place)

Preposisjonen at brukes om steder og begivenheter:

  • He was at school.
  • She was at a handball match.

Preposisjonen in brukes om ute- og inneområder og om byer, land og stater:

  • They had lunch in the garden.
  • We eat in the kitchen.
  • I live in California.

Andre preposisjoner som blir brukt om sted og/eller bevegelse:

above, across, behind, below, beside, between, in front of, near, opposite, over, through, under, up, with

Preposisjonene towards/toward og against

Against kan oversettes med mot og inntil, mens toward/towards oversettes med mot, til; (dvs. i retning av) vendt mot:

  • The dog came running towards me. (Hunden kom springende mot meg.)
  • She stood leaning against a tree. (Hun stod og lente seg mot et tre.)

Preposisjonene in og into

  • The family gathered in the living room. (Familien samlet seg i stua.)
  • Grandma came into the living room. (Bestemor kom inn i rommet.)

Merk at into uttrykker bevegelse fra et sted til et annet.

Språk (Language)

Tekstbindere (Linking words)

Tekstbindere er ord som skaper sammenheng mellom setningene, og er med på å gi teksten god flyt. Det er viktig å bruke bestemte tekstbindere slik at innholdet kommer klart fram. For at ikke setningene skal bli for like hverandre, er det også viktig å variere bruken av tekstbindere.

Eksempler: however, to begin with, also, first, next, later, after, in addition, meanwhile, finally, for example, in fact, as a result, to sum up

Bruk av do i spørrende og nektende setninger

Setninger i vanlig presens eller vanlig preteritum må skrives om med hjelpeverbet do for å lage spørsmål og nektende setninger. Hjelpeverbet do blir bøyd i setningen, og hovedverbet står alltid i infinitiv:

Spørsmål og nektende setninger i presens:

  • Do you speak English?
  • Does he speak English?
  • They do not (don't) speak French.
  • She does not (doesn't) speak French.

Spørsmål og nektende setninger i preteritum:

  • Did you see that bird?
  • I did not (didn't) hear what you said.

Forvekslingsord / vanlige feil (Common mistakes)

Your / you're

your = din/deres Your uttrykker eierskap, at noe tilhører noen.

you're = du er / dere er You're er en sammentrekning av you are.

  • Your backpack is in the classroom.
  • You're a very kind person.

Its / it's

its = dens, dets, sin, sitt Its uttrykker eierskap for noe som ikke er en person.

it's = det er It's er en sammentrekning av it is.

  • The car looks great with its new colour.
  • It's raining outside.

There / their / they're

there = der, dit, det (i betydningen «det er»)

their = deres / sin, sitt, sine

they're = de er, sammentrekning av they are

  • Your pencil case is not here, it is over there.
  • It's their boat. They sold their house.
  • They're coming over tomorrow.

To / two / too

to = infinitivsmerket å, til

two = tallord (2)

too = også, altfor

  • I want to go home.
  • She bought two apples.
  • If you're leaving, I'm leaving too.
  • My hair colour is too dark.

Then / than

then = da, den gang, så

than = enn (than brukes for å uttrykke en sammenlikning)

  • I was still a young man then.
  • We can go to the movies first, then to the restaurant.
  • I am stronger than you.

He is / he's / his

he is / he's = han er He's er en sammentrekning av he is.

his = hans His uttrykker eierskap, at han eier noe.

  • He's an intelligent young man.
  • His room is messy.

To lend / to borrow

to lend = å låne To lend bruker du hvis du skal låne bort noe.

to borrow = å låne To borrow bruker du hvis du skal låne noe fra noen.

  • I can lend you some clothes if you like.
  • Can I borrow your car?

Teach / learn

to teach = å lære bort, å undervise, å instruere

to learn = å lære i betydningen å ta imot undervisning eller instruksjon

  • The teacher said: "I am going to teach you how to write an article."
  • I want to learn about the universe.

Look / see / watch

to see = passiv betydning av å se

(Man ser hele tiden så lenge man er våken og har øynene åpne.)

  • I see a cat outside.

to look = aktiv betydning av å se

(Brukes når man aktivt går inn for å se noe.)

  • Look at this beautiful dress.

to watch = aktiv betydning av å se / å se på

(Brukes når man ser på noe aktivt over tid.)

  • I like to watch television.

Through / though / tough / taught / thought

through = gjennom

though = selv om, men

tough = tøff, hard

taught = underviste

thought = tenkte, trodde, en tanke

  • The car crashed through the shop window.
  • She still argues, though she knows she's wrong.
  • They were a tough crowd to entertain.
  • The teacher taught them how to conjugate irregular verbs.
  • He thought that football practice started at six o'clock.

Lesestrategier (Reading strategies)

Søkelesing (Scanning)

Når man søkeleser, er man ute etter å finne bestemt informasjon i en tekst. Man lar øynene gli raskt over teksten for å finne den bestemte informasjonen. Hvis man for eksempel skal finne ut hvor høyt et fjell er, vil man la øynene gli til man finner et tall, for så å lese nøyere for å finne ut om det er den informasjonen man er ute etter.

Skumlesing (Skimming)

Når man skumleser, leser man raskt gjennom teksten for å finne ut hva teksten handler om i grove trekk. Man merker seg overskrifter, bilder, bildetekst og uthevede ord. Dette er for å finne ut hva som er hovedtrekkene i teksten.

Nærlesing (Close reading)

Når man nærleser en tekst, prøver man å gå detaljert gjennom teksten. Særlig bruker man nærlesing når man leser skjønnlitterære tekster som noveller og dikt. Da forsøker man å se om det ligger noen dypere mening bak teksten. Det kan for eksempel være symboler eller metaforer som kan hjelpe oss å se teksten på en ny måte. Det kan også være for å legge bedre merke til hvilke ord forfatteren har valgt å bruke, eller se hvordan setningene er bygd opp.
